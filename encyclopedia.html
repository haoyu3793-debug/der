The fallow deer of Phoenix Park.

A field guide to Dama dama — the only deer species you'll ever see in the park, here for over 360 years and 600 strong today.

The species

Quick taxonomy and origin

Fallow deer

Dama dama

A medium-sized European deer, originally native to the eastern Mediterranean and Asia Minor. Re-introduced across Europe by the Romans and Normans for hunting parks, and brought to Ireland by Anglo-Norman settlers in the 13th century. Phoenix Park's herd was established by the Duke of Ormonde in the 1660s.

How to identify them

Bucks, does and fawns at a glance

Fallow deer are unmistakable when you know what to look for. Adults stand about 80–95 cm at the shoulder. Their most distinctive feature is the palmate antlers on adult bucks — broad, flattened blades unlike any other deer in Europe. The coat is usually chestnut with bright white spots in summer, fading to grey-brown in winter. A long black tail with a black stripe down the rump and a white patch outlined in black ("inverted horseshoe") is diagnostic.

Bucks (males) grow antlers from age two and are substantially larger than does. Does (females) are antlerless. Fawns are born in June with extra-bright spots, hidden alone in long grass for the first week or two of life.

In fallow-deer terminology, males are properly called bucks (not stags) and females are does (not hinds) — though many Phoenix Park visitors and signs use stag/doe interchangeably.

The four coat morphs

All four occur in the Phoenix Park herd, but in very uneven proportions

~80% of herd

Common

The classic chestnut-brown coat with bright white spots in summer. Spots fade and the coat darkens to grey-brown in winter.

~10% of herd

Menil

Pale sandy coat with white spots that remain visible year-round. No black border on the rump patch. Considered the prettiest morph.

~10% of herd

Melanistic

Dark chocolate-brown to almost black. No spots. Often misidentified as a different species entirely.

Rare

White (leucistic)

Cream to pure white. Not albino — these animals have normal dark eyes. Only a handful in the Phoenix Park herd at any time.

The antler cycle

A complete regrowth — bone, blood vessels and all — every single year

1

Cast

April – May

Old antlers fall off — usually within a few minutes of each other. Bucks look briefly "naked".

2

Velvet growth

May – August

New antlers grow extremely fast (up to 2 cm per day) under a fuzzy skin called velvet, full of blood vessels.

3

Velvet shed

August – September

Velvet dies and is rubbed off against trees. The bare bone underneath is the hardened "trophy" antler.

4

Rut & carry

October – April

Antlers are used for fighting other bucks during the rut, then carried through winter until cast again.

Behaviour & vocalisations

What you'll see and hear in the park

📣

The groan

During the October rut, bucks produce a deep, repetitive belch-like groan that can be heard hundreds of metres away. It establishes territory and intimidates rivals.

⚔️

Sparring & fighting

Bucks lock antlers, push each other across "rutting stands" of trampled ground. Most contests are bluff; serious injuries do occur.

👥

Herd structure

Outside the rut, does and fawns form family groups; bucks live in bachelor groups. They mix only during the autumn rut.

🌿

Diet

Mostly grass, but also browses leaves, acorns, fruit, bark and herbs. The Fifteen Acres grassland sustains the bulk of the herd.

🌅

Active hours

Crepuscular — most active at dawn and dusk. They rest in cover during the middle of the day, especially in summer.

🐾

Hidden fawns

Newborns spend their first week or two lying motionless in long grass while the doe forages nearby. Never touch a "lost" fawn — it isn't lost.

Glossary of deer terms

Words you'll hear from rangers and birders

Buck
Adult male fallow deer (≈ "stag" in red-deer terminology).
Doe
Adult female fallow deer (≈ "hind" in red-deer terminology).
Fawn
A deer in its first year of life.
Pricket
A young buck (about 1 year old) growing its first antlers — straight unbranched spikes.
Palmate
Antler shape resembling an open hand — broad and flattened. Unique to fallow and moose among European deer.
Velvet
The skin covering growing antlers, full of blood vessels. Shed in late summer.
Cast
To shed antlers (verb). Cast antlers can be found on the ground in spring.
Rut
The annual mating season. For fallow deer, the whole of October.
Groan
The bellowing call of a fallow buck during the rut — distinctive, deep and repetitive.
Crepuscular
Active at dawn and dusk rather than day or night.
Bachelor group
A group of bucks living together outside the rut.
Harem
Group of does a dominant buck gathers and defends during the rut.

Other deer found in Ireland

For context — none of these live in Phoenix Park

Not in Phoenix Park

Red deer

Cervus elaphus

Ireland's only truly native deer, in a continuous lineage since the last ice age. The pure-bred herd in Killarney National Park is the largest. Stags reach 200 kg. Branched (not palmate) antlers.

Not in Phoenix Park

Sika deer

Cervus nippon

Introduced from Japan to Powerscourt, Co. Wicklow in 1860 by Lord Powerscourt. Spread widely across Wicklow, Donegal and Kerry. Hybridise readily with red deer, threatening the pure red-deer line.

Not in Phoenix Park

Reeves's muntjac

Muntiacus reevesi

Small dog-sized deer native to China. A recent invasive species in Ireland, first confirmed in Wicklow in the 2000s. Currently considered a serious biosecurity concern. None recorded in Phoenix Park.
